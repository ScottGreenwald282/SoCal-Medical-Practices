September 29, 2026 March 30, 2026 by Brandon Miller, NMLS# 1129696 DSCR Loan Limits — The Quick Read: There&#8217;s no government cap on a DSCR loan. Most files in the wholesale network land between roughly $100,000 and $3,000,000, with leverage running 75%-80% on a purchase and topping out around 75% on a cash-out refinance. Above that standard ceiling, select lenders go higher on a case-by-case basis. The real number you can borrow depends on the property&#8217;s rent, its appraised value, your credit tier, and how those combine into a coverage ratio — not on your paycheck. That last part is the whole point of a debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR) loan. Instead of checking your W-2s and traditional personal-income documentation, a lender checks whether the property&#8217;s rent covers its own payment. This is a business-purpose loan — meaning it&#8217;s made to an investor buying or refinancing rental property, not a home you live in — so it gets underwritten differently from a regular mortgage. Is There an Official DSCR Loan Limit? No. There&#8217;s no regulatory ceiling on DSCR loan amounts, because these loans never go to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. The conforming loan limit — a number that DOES cap agency-backed conventional loans — simply doesn&#8217;t apply here. For reference, the Federal Housing Finance Agency sets that conforming loan limit at $832,750 for most one-unit properties, with a high-cost ceiling of $1,249,125. Investors sometimes assume that number governs every mortgage in the country. It doesn&#8217;t govern DSCR loans at all. DSCR loans sell into private capital markets, not to the government-sponsored enterprises, so each lender in the network sets its own ceiling instead of following one federal rule. In practice, that means &#8220;how much can I borrow&#8221; isn&#8217;t a fixed answer — it&#8217;s a formula. Rent, DSCR ratio, loan-to-value (LTV, the percentage of the property&#8217;s value the loan covers), credit score, and the appraisal all combine to produce your number. Two investors buying identical $400,000 duplexes can land on very different maximum loan amounts depending on the rent roll and their credit profile. How Much Can You Actually Borrow? Most standard DSCR files in the wholesale network run from around $100,000 up to $3,000,000, with purchase leverage typically sitting between 75% and 80% loan-to-value. Select high-leverage programs push to 85% LTV — about 15% down — generally for borrowers with credit scores around 700 or higher. Here&#8217;s the mechanic that matters more than the ceiling itself: the property has to clear a minimum coverage ratio, and a bigger down payment doesn&#8217;t override that. Coverage compares monthly rent to the full monthly housing payment — principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues, sometimes abbreviated PITIA. On most standard programs, that ratio needs to clear 1.00 — meaning rent covers the payment dollar for dollar — though 1.00 is a floor on select programs, not a universal rule everywhere in the market. Clearing 1.00 does not mean the property is cash-flow positive in the way most investors think about it. DSCR only measures rent against the mortgage payment. It ignores repairs, vacancy, property management fees, utilities, and capital expenses. A property that clears 1.00 could still lose money once those real costs get added in. Treat DSCR as a lending threshold, not a profitability test. A larger down payment helps in two ways: it lowers the monthly payment, which can push your coverage ratio higher, and it reduces the lender&#8217;s exposure. But it never overrides a leverage cap, a credit floor, a reserve requirement, or a property-type restriction. The strongest files clear both hurdles — enough equity in the deal AND enough rent to cover the payment. These specifics are subject to lender guidelines and a full review of property, leverage, and credit. What Actually Sets Your Ceiling? Four things: your credit score, the property&#8217;s appraised value, the coverage ratio, and the leverage tier for that loan size. Change any one of them, and your maximum loan amount moves with it. Terms vary by lender guidelines, property type, leverage, credit profile, and full file review. Credit score. A 620 floor exists on parts of the network, but most programs want something closer to 660. Scores of 700 and above unlock the strongest leverage tiers — meaning less money down for the same purchase price. Appraised value and rent. This is where the file actually gets built. Appraisers use a Fannie Mae convention to document market rent even on non-agency DSCR loans — for a one-unit property, that&#8217;s Form 1007, the Single-Family Comparable Rent Schedule , which pulls comparable rental data to support the rent figure the lender uses. For 2-4 unit properties, a similar operating-income statement documents the rent roll across all units. If the appraiser&#8217;s rent estimate comes in low, your maximum loan amount comes down with it — no matter what a rental listing site says the unit could fetch. Exact terms depend on the lender&#8217;s guidelines, property type, leverage, and a full review of the borrower&#8217;s file. Reserves. Lenders want to see liquid assets left over after closing, typically measured in months of PITIA. On most files that&#8217;s around 6 months. Above roughly $1,500,000, that commonly steps up to around 9 months. Reserve requirements vary by lender, leverage, and transaction type, so treat these as typical ranges rather than fixed rules — some conservative rate-and-term files at modest leverage under $1,500,000 have seen reserves waived entirely. Leverage on cash-out. If you&#8217;re refinancing to pull equity out rather than buying, the ceiling drops. Cash-out refinances on standard rental property generally cap around 75% LTV across most of the network, and lenders typically want to see about 6 months of seasoning — meaning you&#8217;ve owned the property that long — before they&#8217;ll consider it. Compare that to a 70% cash-out ceiling on short-term-rental collateral specifically, which runs lower because the income stream is considered less stable. What About Loans Below or Above 1.00 Coverage? Sub-1.00 coverage and no-ratio structures both exist through select lenders — they&#8217;re real paths, not dead ends, but they come with tradeoffs. A property that doesn&#8217;t quite clear 1.00 on paper isn&#8217;t automatically unfinanceable. Select programs in the network will still consider it, generally at reduced leverage and adjusted terms to offset the weaker coverage. No-ratio loans go a step further — no coverage ratio calculated at all. These are generally reserved for borrowers who already own a primary residence, and they&#8217;re only available through select lenders, so don&#8217;t expect this on every file. Neither of these paths carries a numeric floor the way standard DSCR programs do; eligibility comes down to the individual lender&#8217;s overlay and the rest of the borrower&#8217;s profile. Does Property Type Change the Limit? Yes — significantly, in two directions. Short-term rentals get their own set of numbers, and a few property types are excluded from the network&#8217;s DSCR programs entirely. For STR purchases, leverage tops out around 75% LTV, with a coverage floor around 1.00. Refinance leverage on an STR generally runs closer to 70%, also with roughly a 1.00 floor — worth noting because purchase and refinance leverage on short-term rentals are not the same number, and lenders typically want to see around 12 months of hosting history plus a credit score of 640 or better before they&#8217;ll count that income at all. On the exclusion side, manufactured homes (both single-wide and double-wide), log homes, and barndominiums are not offered under the network&#8217;s DSCR programs. If you&#8217;re eyeing one of these, it falls outside this financing category — that&#8217;s a program limitation, not a reflection of the property&#8217;s investment potential. One more limit worth knowing: investment-property HELOC lines (a home equity line of credit against a rental you already own) cap at $500,000 total across the network. There&#8217;s no higher tier above that for investor HELOCs specifically. DSCR vs. conventional financing Two common ways to finance an investment property in this market. They qualify you differently — here’s how investors weigh them. DSCR loan Why investors choose it Qualifies on the property’s rental income — no personal tax returns, W-2s, or pay stubs needed to document income. No personal debt-to-income ceiling to clear, so existing mortgages and obligations don’t cap your borrowing the same way. Can be closed in an LLC, keeping the property inside a business entity. Built for scaling — not held to the limit on number of financed properties that conventional financing applies. Underwriting centers on the deal: generally qualifies when the rent covers the payment, a 1.00x coverage ratio being a common baseline (confirmed in underwriting). Designed specifically for investment property, including long-term and, where the program allows, short-term rentals. Conventional loan Where it’s strong Often the lowest ongoing financing cost for a buyer who fully qualifies on personal income — a fit for a first property or a cost-first purchase. Trade-offs for investors Requires full personal income documentation and must fit within a debt-to-income limit — salary, existing debts, and other mortgages all count. Typically held in your personal name rather than a business entity. Caps how many financed properties you can carry, which can become a ceiling as a portfolio grows. Evaluates you as a borrower as much as the property, which usually means more paperwork. How investors usually choose: a first or single property often optimizes for the lowest financing cost; portfolio builders often optimize for leverage, vesting in an LLC, and scaling past conventional caps. The right answer depends on your goals, the property, and current guidelines — both paths run through select lenders in Lendmire’s wholesale network, with eligibility and terms confirmed in underwriting. Above $2,500,000, the network generally holds to 30-year fixed structures rather than adjustable or interest-only options. And in overlay states — Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, and New Jersey are the ones that show up most often with tighter rules — purchase leverage generally caps closer to 75% LTV, and loan amounts in those states commonly top out around $2,000,000. Across the broader market, non-QM lending (the category DSCR loans fall under) has grown fast enough that this isn&#8217;t a fringe product anymore. Investor and DSCR loans made up 35% of non-QM production as of a recent measure, up from 22% a few years earlier, and lock volume in that category rose 130% over a similar stretch, according to HousingWire . Credit quality tracks conventional lending closer than most people assume — the average non-QM borrower carried a 776 FICO score in one recent measure, per Scotsman Guide , which undercuts the idea that skipping income docs means skipping underwriting standards. Every figure here varies by lender and program — guidelines, property type, leverage, and credit profile all apply. Files brokered across a wide range of DSCR lenders tend to show a pattern: the file that gets stuck isn&#8217;t usually the one with a weak credit score — it&#8217;s the one where the appraiser&#8217;s rent estimate comes in below what the investor expected. Building in a rent cushion before you make an offer, rather than assuming top-of-market comps will hold, tends to keep the loan amount from shrinking mid-file. Key Terms Defined DSCR (debt-service coverage ratio): monthly rent divided by the full monthly housing payment — a number above 1.00 means rent covers the payment. LTV (loan-to-value): the loan amount as a percentage of the property&#8217;s appraised value; 80% LTV means a 20% down payment. Final terms depend on lender guidelines, property type, leverage, and the borrower&#8217;s complete credit picture. PITIA: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues combined — the full monthly obligation used in the DSCR calculation. Seasoning: the length of time you must own a property before a lender will consider a cash-out refinance on it. Business-purpose loan: financing for an investment property rather than a home you live in, which is why DSCR loans are reviewed and disclosed differently than a standard owner-occupied mortgage. Frequently Asked Questions Is there a minimum DSCR loan amount? Most standard files in the wholesale network start around $100,000, though smaller balances often route through select lenders rather than the core program. If your deal is below that, expect fewer lender options and possibly different terms. Can I get a DSCR loan if my coverage ratio is below 1.00? Yes, through select lenders — sub-1.00 coverage is a real path, generally with reduced leverage to offset the weaker ratio. It&#8217;s not available on every program, and terms adjust to compensate for the lower coverage. Does a bigger down payment let me borrow more? It raises your DSCR ratio by lowering the payment, and it reduces the lender&#8217;s risk — both of which help. But it doesn&#8217;t remove a leverage cap, a credit-score floor, or a reserve requirement. You still need to clear those independently. Why does the appraisal matter so much for my loan amount? Because the appraiser&#8217;s rent estimate — documented on forms modeled after Fannie Mae&#8217;s rent schedule — sets the income figure your coverage ratio is built on. If that rent number comes in low, your maximum loan amount comes down with it, regardless of what you expected the unit to rent for. Can DSCR loans finance a manufactured home or barndominium? No. Manufactured homes, log homes, and barndominiums fall outside the network&#8217;s DSCR programs entirely. That&#8217;s a program exclusion, not a case-by-case credit decision. Investors weighing an interest-only structure specifically should also look at how much you can borrow on an interest-only DSCR loan , since interest-only payments change the coverage math differently than a standard amortizing loan.
